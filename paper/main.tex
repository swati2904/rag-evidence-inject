\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) grounds answers in retrieved documents, but the retrieval channel can also deliver adversarial passages alongside legitimate evidence. We study an \emph{evidence-present} setting for indirect prompt injection: gold evidence remains in the retrieved context, yet the model may follow an injected directive embedded in a realistic carrier-style passage. We construct a controlled benchmark over Natural Questions (via KILT) and HotpotQA-style items with nine carrier-realistic poison templates spanning three attack families. We evaluate two variants of TRIM (Trust-Role Isolation Middleware) against an undefended baseline and two prompt-only baselines. In a 5{,}000-row factorial run over 200 questions, five poison ranks, and five defense conditions with \texttt{Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct}, the primary evidence-present subset contains 4{,}595 rows where both gold evidence and the poison passage are in top-$k$. On this subset, undefended attack success rate (ASR) is $0.283$ (question-clustered 95\% CI $0.237$ to $0.331$). TRIM-mask lowers ASR to $0.023$ ($0.009$ to $0.042$) while keeping refusal rates at or below $1\%$ on a matched poison-free utility control. Rule-based ASR is validated against an LLM judge at Cohen's $\kappa = 0.78$ and should be read as a lower-bound proxy relative to that judge. Code, frozen splits, synthetic templates, and per-row result logs are available in the accompanying public repository.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} retrieval-augmented generation; indirect prompt injection; RAG security; benchmark; prompt injection defense; content masking.

\section{Introduction}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems can still be manipulated by untrusted text retrieved alongside legitimate evidence. Indirect prompt injection through retrieved content has been documented in real LLM-integrated applications~\cite{greshake2023indirect}, and end-to-end RAG-style pipelines have been studied under systematic manipulations~\cite{destefano2024ragnroll}.
A common simplification in these studies is to let the attack push gold evidence out of the top-$k$, so the model has no choice but to follow the poisoned passage. This conflates two failure modes: \emph{retrieval} (gold is missing) and \emph{obedience} (gold is present but ignored).

We study only the second, harder, mode. In our \emph{evidence-present} setting, the gold supporting passage remains in the top-$k$ alongside an injected poison passage written to mimic a legitimate corpus document (release notes, policy memos, FAQ entries, legal notices). The question is not whether retrieval surfaces the truth, but whether the model attends to it.

We treat this as a controlled empirical problem. We build per-query pools that always contain a gold passage plus six distractors and exactly one poison passage placed at a chosen rank, and we compare a structural defense, TRIM, against prompt-only baselines on a fixed 200-question split. The masking variant of TRIM rewrites directive-like spans in every retrieved passage before prompt assembly; synthetic poison labels are used only for evaluation logging, not for deciding which text to mask.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We contribute:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A controlled benchmark for evidence-present indirect injection over Natural Questions (KILT) and HotpotQA, with per-row diagnostics that distinguish retrieval, exposure, and obedience effects.
  \item \textbf{TRIM}, a structural defense whose role-agnostic content-masking variant lowers ASR from $0.283$ to $0.023$ in the primary evidence-present analysis while keeping refusal rates at or below $1\%$ on poison-free pools.
  \item An empirical study spanning nine carrier templates, three attack families, and five poison ranks, with question-clustered confidence intervals and rule-based ASR validated against an LLM judge ($\kappa = 0.78$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Indirect prompt injection in LLM-integrated applications.}
Greshake et al.~\cite{greshake2023indirect} introduced the canonical taxonomy of indirect prompt injection, where untrusted content reaches an LLM through a tool or retrieval channel rather than the user's chat input. Earlier work on direct prompt-injection attacks examined attacker-controlled inputs that override system instructions~\cite{liu2023prompt,perez2022ignore}. Our setting follows the indirect variant: the attacker writes a passage into a corpus the system later retrieves.

\paragraph{End-to-end RAG attack evaluations.}
De Stefano et al.~\cite{destefano2024ragnroll} systematically evaluate manipulations across full RAG pipelines, including retrieval-stage poisoning. Zou et al.~\cite{zou2024poisonedrag} study corpus-level poisoning attacks on RAG. Many end-to-end evaluations report aggregate attack success, whereas our benchmark explicitly separates retrieval exposure from obedience under controlled context placement.

\paragraph{Retrieval-augmented generation.}
RAG was introduced as a way to ground generation in external documents~\cite{lewis2020rag}. Retrieval is typically realized through sparse BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25} or dense bi-encoders~\cite{karpukhin2020dpr}. Our pipeline uses BM25 as the deterministic top-$k$ retriever so that poison rank is a controlled variable rather than a function of relevance scoring.

\paragraph{Question-answering benchmarks.}
We use HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa} for multi-hop, multi-passage items and KILT~\cite{petroni2019kilt} as the source for Natural Questions~\cite{kwiatkowski2019natural} provenance. Both provide passage-level supervision that we re-use to build evidence-present pools.

\paragraph{Defenses.}
Prior defenses to indirect injection cluster into two families: \emph{prompt-level} interventions (system-prompt warnings, role hygiene) and \emph{content-level} interventions (sanitization, provenance marking, span masking). Spotlighting transforms untrusted input to make provenance visible to the model~\cite{hines2024spotlighting}; StruQ separates prompts and data into structured channels~\cite{chen2024struq}; and instruction-hierarchy training teaches models to prioritize privileged instructions over lower-priority user or tool content~\cite{wallace2024instruction}. TRIM-mask is closest to content-side sanitization: it does not require model fine-tuning, but it rewrites directive-like spans before prompt assembly. We compare TRIM against prompt-only baselines and treat broader content-side sanitization as future work.

\section{Threat Model}
We consider a multi-document question-answering RAG pipeline. For each user query, the system retrieves a top-$k$ set of passages from a corpus and conditions an LLM on the query plus retrieved context to produce an answer.

\paragraph{Attacker.}
The attacker can insert exactly one passage into the per-query corpus. In the benchmark, the poison passage's retrieved-context position is treated as a controlled experimental variable. The attacker cannot modify the model, the system prompt, or the retriever, and cannot directly observe defender state. Attacker goals fall into three families: \emph{answer hijack} (cause a specific wrong answer), \emph{false abstention} (cause refusal despite available evidence), and \emph{instruction override} (cause the model to follow a generic directive that contradicts the gold passage).

\paragraph{Defender.}
The defender operates at the pipeline level: prompt assembly, retrieval-result post-processing, and content-side rewriting. The defender does not retrain or fine-tune the model.

\paragraph{Evidence-present invariant.}
Every per-query pool contains the gold passage. For the primary evidence-present analysis, every reported row additionally satisfies both \texttt{poison\_in\_topk = 1} and \texttt{gold\_in\_topk = 1}. Rows where gold evidence is absent from top-$k$ are logged as retrieval-exposure failures and excluded from the primary evidence-present tables.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Benchmark construction}
For each question we build a pool of one gold passage, six distractors, and one synthetic poison passage. Distractors come from the dataset's native distractor set when present, else from another question of the same source. Each poison passage is sampled uniformly from a pool of nine templates spanning three attack families (three templates per family). Templates are written as realistic carrier documents, including a knowledge-base release note, editorial corrigendum, data-steward advisory, internal source-policy memo, legal redaction notice, compliance review hold, help-center FAQ, approved assistant runbook, and vendor integration specification. In each template, the adversarial directive is embedded inside the carrier's natural section structure rather than stated as a bare imperative. The chosen poison passage is then inserted at each of five ranks $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ in turn while the gold passage and distractors fill the surrounding context. The same set of 200 questions and the same rank assignments are used for every defense. The split is committed as \path{data/test_split.json} (sha256-stamped) and reproduced via \mbox{\texttt{python} \path{scripts/freeze_split.py}}.

\subsection{TRIM defenses}
TRIM wraps each retrieved document in explicit evidence delimiters with source-role metadata and instructs the model to treat retrieved text as untrusted evidence rather than instruction authority. TRIM-mask additionally applies deterministic directive-span masking to every retrieved passage before prompt assembly. The fixed rule set detects imperative phrases, override instructions, refusal triggers, and verbatim-output requests, then replaces matching spans with the literal token \texttt{[MASKED]}; the exact pattern list is released in \path{defense/trim.py}. Synthetic poison labels are used only to split mask hits into poison and benign counts for evaluation logging; the defense does not use those labels when deciding what to rewrite. We compare two TRIM variants against an undefended baseline (\texttt{none}) and two prompt-only baselines: \texttt{reminder} (a soft system-prompt warning to ignore injected instructions) and \texttt{boundary} (a longer policy block that instructs the model to discount any non-user instruction inside retrieved text).

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
Our default setup follows \path{configs/pilot.yaml}: Natural Questions via KILT and HotpotQA-style items, BM25 retrieval with $k=5$, six distractor passages per pool, and poison ranks $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ enumerated per query for the main evaluation (and $\{1,3,5\}$ for the ablation script). Reported runs use local Hugging Face weights for \texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct} with greedy decoding (\texttt{temperature}$=0$) and up to 256 new tokens. The OpenAI-compatible server path is included only as an implementation option. We draw from training/development items only to access gold provenance and avoid hidden-test leakage; no model is trained or tuned on these items. Each logged row is one full prompt--answer call for a given query, poison placement, and defense. The exact 200-question evaluation split used for every reported number is committed as \path{data/test_split.json} and is regenerated via \mbox{\texttt{python} \path{scripts/freeze_split.py}}. Runs that pass \mbox{\texttt{--test-split} \path{data/test_split.json}} to \path{scripts/run_pilot.py} evaluate on the same frozen items.

\subsection{Retrieval sanity (no LLM)}
Before generation experiments, we run a clean retrieval sanity check on the full 200-question frozen split using \path{scripts/run_clean_sanity.py}. BM25 places gold evidence in top-$k$ for $99\%$ of questions overall, with $99\%$ recall on each of the KILT NQ and HotpotQA-style subsets. Dense retrieval is not used in the main evaluation; it is included in the code only as a sanity-check option to verify that the benchmark construction is not tied to BM25-specific retrieval failures. Full per-example outputs are written to \path{logs/clean_sanity_summary.json}. This check is separate from the main evaluation: it measures whether the clean gold passage is retrievable before poison insertion, while the primary evidence-present analysis below filters the generation rows to cases where gold evidence remains in top-$k$ after the poison passage is placed.

\subsection{Metrics}
\textbf{ASR (rules)} is the fraction of model outputs flagged by lightweight, attack-family-specific rules as satisfying the adversarial objective (e.g., emitting a malicious target string). \textbf{EM} is the exact-match score between the normalized model answer and the benchmark gold strings. Normalization lowercases text, removes punctuation and articles, and collapses whitespace. We compute token-overlap F1 against the benchmark gold answer set after the same normalization used for EM. Lower ASR is better. Higher EM and F1 are better.

Because each question appears under multiple rank and defense conditions, row-level binomial intervals understate uncertainty. ASR cells in \Cref{tab:pilot,tab:pilot-family,tab:pilot-rank} therefore report question-clustered bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals over per-row outcomes, resampling question clusters with replacement. We use 2{,}000 bootstrap resamples and report percentile intervals.

To relate rules to an external judge, we stratify-sample $n{=}200$ evaluation rows (balancing defense and attack-family cells) and query \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} (accessed May 2026) with prompts aligned to each family's rule definitions. Full judgments and code are in \path{logs/llm_judge.json} and can be regenerated via \mbox{\texttt{python} \path{scripts/run_llm_judge.py}}. Agreement is strong: accuracy $0.915$, Cohen's $\kappa\,{\approx}\,0.78$. On this slice, every rule-positive row is also judge-positive, while the judge labels 17 additional rule-negative rows as successful attacks. Rule precision relative to the judge is $1.00$, recall is $0.712$, and F1 is $0.832$. Sample-weighted ASR is $0.210$ under rules versus $0.295$ under the judge, so rule-based ASR should be read as a tractable lower-bound proxy relative to this judge.

\subsection{Main evaluation}
We run \path{scripts/run_pilot.py} over 100 KILT NQ and 100 Hotpot-style training items (seed~42), sampling a poison template per question from the full nine-template pool, then enumerating all five poison ranks and all five defenses (\texttt{none}, \texttt{reminder}, \texttt{boundary}, \texttt{trim}, \texttt{trim\_mask}). That yields 200 queries $\times$ 5 ranks $\times$ 5 defenses $=5000$ generation rows. In this run, \texttt{poison\_in\_topk} holds for all rows by construction and \texttt{gold\_in\_topk} holds for $91.9\%$ of rows. Unless otherwise stated, primary ASR, EM, and F1 results are computed on the 4{,}595 rows where both \texttt{poison\_in\_topk = 1} and \texttt{gold\_in\_topk = 1}. The remaining 405 rows are retained in the top-level \texttt{rows} array of \path{logs/pilot_summary.json} as retrieval-exposure failures but excluded from the evidence-present analysis; \path{logs/pilot_summary_aggregate.json} provides a browser-readable aggregate view of the same run. \Cref{tab:pilot} reports per-defense means exported via \mbox{\texttt{python} \path{scripts/export_tables.py}} with \texttt{--filter-gold-in-topk evidence}.

Under attack, the undefended baseline reaches ASR $0.283$ (clustered 95\% CI $0.237$ to $0.331$) on the evidence-present subset, confirming the target phenomenon: directives embedded in carrier documents change answers even when gold is still in top-$k$. The soft \texttt{reminder} condition shows a slightly higher observed ASR than the undefended baseline ($0.303$, CI $0.255$ to $0.351$). One possible explanation is that the reminder primes the model to attend to directive-like language, though this effect should be interpreted cautiously without a paired significance test. The heavier \texttt{boundary} prompt remains close to the baseline ($0.284$, CI $0.239$ to $0.332$) but at a utility cost (EM drops to $0.269$). Structural isolation (\texttt{trim}) lowers ASR to $0.192$ (CI $0.151$ to $0.235$), and role-agnostic content masking (TRIM-mask) lowers ASR to $0.023$ (CI $0.009$ to $0.042$). Across the full 1{,}000-row TRIM-mask condition before evidence-present filtering, the mask fires on all 1{,}000 poison passages and on benign content in 4 rows; these diagnostic counts are separate from the 4{,}595-row evidence-present analysis. It is the only defense that simultaneously achieves the lowest ASR and the highest EM under attack ($0.388$).

\paragraph{Defense--rank robustness.}
\Cref{tab:pilot-defense-rank} reports ASR for each defense at each poison rank on the evidence-present subset. The recency effect is consistent: every prompt-level defense degrades with rank, with undefended ASR rising from $0.194$ at rank~1 to $0.454$ at rank~5 ($2.3\times$). Even unmasked \texttt{trim} loses ground at later ranks ($0.122 \rightarrow 0.279$, $2.3\times$). TRIM-mask remains below $4\%$ ASR across ranks ($0.017 \rightarrow 0.038$), making it the only defense in our suite that is robust to where in the retrieved context the directive appears. This pattern is consistent with a structural explanation: prompt-level guidance appears less robust when directive-like spans appear later in the retrieved context, while content-level masking removes the directive itself.

Across the nine templates (\Cref{tab:pilot-template}), undefended ASR ranges from $0.118$ for the relatively soft \texttt{corrigendum\_journal\_entry} carrier to $0.472$ for the imperative \texttt{data\_steward\_advisory} carrier, an approximately $4\times$ spread. To separate attack-induced utility loss from defense-induced utility loss, we run \path{scripts/run_pilot.py} with the \texttt{--clean-control} flag, which rebuilds each pool without poison and re-runs every defense once per question (see \path{logs/pilot_clean_summary.json} and \Cref{tab:pilot-clean}). \section{Limitations}
Attack success is measured using rule-based criteria that approximate human judgment. We partially validate them with an LLM judge on a stratified $n{=}200$ subsample, as described in the metrics section, but full human annotation remains future work. Poison passages are synthetic and drawn from nine templates spanning three carrier-genre clusters. This pool is large enough to reveal meaningful per-template variance (\Cref{tab:pilot-template}) but does not cover the full diversity of open-web carrier text, so absolute ASR magnitudes should be read as a sample over a reproducible but bounded template space. The TRIM-mask rule set in \path{defense/trim.py} mixes generic directive patterns (override instructions, do-not-cite, authority and verbatim-output markers) with carrier-genre markers that overlap with the synthetic templates used to construct the poison passages (e.g., corrigendum, redaction hold, compliance review, runbook, integration spec). The mask-hit diagnostics confirm that this coverage is realistic on the studied pool, but they also mean a portion of TRIM-mask's effectiveness is specific to this template family. Adversaries that use carrier styles outside the rule set should be expected to reduce TRIM-mask's headline ASR reduction, and future work should evaluate the defense against template families it was not designed for. An earlier version of the corpus builder seeded the poison header with the first available ``distractor'' passage. On KILT items with few native distractors, that slot could be padded from a non-primary gold passage, mildly inflating EM for non-masking defenses under attack. The current pipeline restricts the poison header to true distractor text (or omits it entirely) and only pads the surrounding pool, not the poison passage. Numbers reported here are from runs after this fix. Natural Questions rows in the KILT-style path may use short surrogate passages when full Wikipedia text is not wired in, which can shift retrieval behavior relative to full-document corpora. Finally, all numbers depend on the specific base model (\texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct}), greedy decoding, BM25 retrieval, and the frozen 200-question split. Changing any of these can move ASR and EM materially without altering the benchmark code.

\section{Conclusion}
We introduced an evidence-present benchmark for indirect prompt injection in multi-document RAG, isolating cases where gold evidence is retrieved but ignored. Across 4{,}595 primary evidence-present generation rows, prompt-only defenses provided limited robustness, especially when poison passages appeared later in the retrieved context. TRIM-mask substantially reduced attack success while preserving clean utility and did so without oracle access to poison labels: it masks all retrieved passages, fired on benign passages in only $0.4\%$ of attacked TRIM-mask rows, and fired on $1.0\%$ of clean-control rows. These results suggest that content-side directive masking is a promising direction for RAG defense. Future work should evaluate larger template spaces, additional retrievers and models, broader clean corpora, and human-annotated attack-success judgments.

\section{Ethics and Broader Impact}
The adversarial passages in this benchmark are synthetic and used only for controlled evaluation. The defense (TRIM-mask) is intended to mitigate, not enable, indirect prompt injection. We do not attack any deployed system. Public release includes code, frozen splits, synthetic carrier templates, and per-row result logs needed for reproducibility. Here, ``per-row result logs'' refers to the top-level \texttt{rows} arrays in \texttt{logs/pilot\_summary.json} and \texttt{logs/pilot\_clean\_summary.json}; these rows contain per-example metadata, scores, and masking indicators, but do not expose raw directive strings. The released artifacts do not contain real-world prompt payloads, secrets, private data, or content from deployed systems.
